\title{The Limits of Test-Time Compute in Iterative Denoisers:\\
A Depth Law, a Substitution Frontier, and Two Things It Does \emph{Not} Buy}
\author{Hafez Al-Khatib \\ American University of Beirut}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
How many refinement steps should an iterative denoiser take? We measure what test-time computation buys
for energy-based models, score networks, and diffusion-style denoisers, and where its value stops. The
optimal depth follows a power law, $K^{*}(\sigma)=C\sigma^{\alpha}$. The form of this law is robust: it
holds across three datasets, five architectures, and a three-thousandfold range of model size
($R^{2}>0.98$). The exponent is not universal, however. It depends on the measurement protocol and the
training regime, ranging from $1.13$ to $1.61$ across our settings. In practice one calibration point is
enough, and using it costs at most $0.12$\,dB relative to oracle depth. The law also has a boundary:
prior-only descent converts steps into quality for stochastic corruptions but not for deterministic ones
such as blur, unless the model is trained on them. Under a criterion registered before data collection,
depth substitutes for capacity at a fixed compute budget. A 32K-parameter energy head given extra descent
steps beats a $3.4\times$ costlier head by up to $3.9$\,dB at matched budgets, while the costlier head's
best advantage at any budget is $0.12$\,dB, a gap that replicates across three training seeds and does
not grow when heads are scaled to 30M parameters. Oversized energy heads are a poor use of inference
compute wherever the law leaves depth to trade. We close with two negative results: adapting depth per
image is worth at most $0.13$\,dB even with oracle stopping, and refinement erases the energy score's
ability to flag out-of-distribution inputs when corruption is light, though the ordering reverses when
it is heavy.
Classical semiconvergence theory explains why quality rises and then decays, but not the sign or the
value of the exponent. That mechanism remains open.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The dominant story in inference-time scaling is optimistic: let the model ``think longer'' and it does
better---in language reasoning \citep{snell2024}, in energy-based transformers \citep{gladstone2025}, in
diffusion refinement. Iterative denoisers are the cleanest place to study this claim quantitatively: an
energy-based model runs gradient descent on a learned energy $E_\theta(\mathbf{x})$, and additional steps
$K$ can improve or, past a point, degrade the estimate.

We give the operational law \emph{and} its boundary. Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A calibrated depth law.} $K^{*}(\sigma)=C\sigma^{\alpha}$ holds in form with $R^2>0.98$
across datasets, architectures, and $3000\times$ in scale; the exponent is protocol- and
regime-conditional ($1.13$--$1.61$ across all measured settings). One measurement calibrates the curve at
a delivered-quality cost of at most $0.12$\,dB (Sec.~\ref{sec:law}).
\item \textbf{Iteration, not architecture.} A feedforward denoiser is exactly flat in $K$: the capability
is intrinsic to iterating a gradient field (Sec.~\ref{sec:iter}).
\item \textbf{A phase boundary.} The law is corruption-conditioned---it holds for stochastic corruptions and
breaks for deterministic ones ($K^{*}\!\approx\!0$), delimiting when test-time compute helps at all
(Sec.~\ref{sec:phase}).
\item \textbf{A substitution frontier.} At fixed inference FLOPs, and under a criterion pre-registered
before data collection, a lighter energy head taking more descent steps beats a $3.4\times$ costlier head
by $+0.8$ to $+3.9$\,dB for $\sigma\geq0.10$ in both seeds---and the costlier head's peak advantage at
\emph{any} budget is $\leq0.12$\,dB, replicated across three training-seed pairs and unchanged by scaling
heads to $30$M parameters, so oversized heads are Pareto-dominated. At $\sigma=0.05$,
$K^{*}\!\approx\!1$ leaves no depth to trade and the heads tie (Sec.~\ref{sec:frontier}).
\item \textbf{Two negative results.} Oracle per-image depth control gains at most $0.13$\,dB over the
noise-level rule (Sec.~\ref{sec:adaptive}), and refinement erases the separability of the energy score on
lightly corrupted inputs, with an honest reversal under heavy corruption (Sec.~\ref{sec:ood-sub}). Both
correct natural hypotheses and both are, we argue, the more useful part of the paper.
\end{enumerate}
We deliberately foreground the limits: the positive law is anticipated in form (though, notably, not in
sign) by classical early-stopping theory, whereas the boundary---what ``thinking longer'' cannot do for an
iterative denoiser---is less appreciated.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Iterative regularization.} Early-stopped iteration is a spectral regularizer with
semiconvergence---quality rises, then decays
\citep{landweber1951,engl1996,hansen2010,yao2007,raskutti2014}. That theory predicts our rise-then-decay;
it does not predict our scaling's \emph{sign}: classical stopping indices \emph{decrease} with noise
(Landweber-type rates stop earlier when data are noisier), whereas our $K^{*}$ increases with $\sigma$,
because our iteration starts \emph{at} the noisy observation and descends toward a learned prior
manifold---the step count is a transport length, not a classical regularization parameter. The
$K^{*}(\sigma)$ scaling in this setting is, to our knowledge, an empirical finding; a spectral mechanism
we tested for it did not survive (Sec.~\ref{sec:limitations}).
\paragraph{Iterative denoisers.} EBMs \citep{lecun2006,du2019}, score matching \citep{song2019}, and
diffusion \citep{ho2020,song2021} share a gradient-refinement operator, explaining why one law spans them.
\paragraph{Denoisers as priors; learned iterative schemes.} Gradient descent on a learned image prior is
the engine of plug-and-play methods \citep{venkatakrishnan2013}, regularization-by-denoising
\citep{romano2017}, and the implicit-prior descent of \citet{kadkhodaie2021}, whose step schedule already
adapts to an estimated effective noise level. Tweedie's formula \citep{efron2011} explains our
$K^{*}{\approx}1$ endpoint (one score step of size $\sigma^{2}$ is the posterior mean), deep image prior
\citep{ulyanov2018} made early-stopped semiconvergence in neural denoising central, and LISTA
\citep{gregor2010} established the depth-vs-quality economics of learned iterative schemes. Relative to
these, we contribute the measured exponent, its cross-architecture statistics, a one-point calibration
rule with a quantified quality cost, and the limits.
\paragraph{Step budgets at scale.} The step-count/quality trade is studied intensively for diffusion:
sampler and schedule design \citep{songddim2021,karras2022,lu2022}, and distillation, which spends
training compute to \emph{remove} steps \citep{salimans2022,songcm2023}---the converse of our
substitution result. Fixed-budget allocation between model size and computation echoes compute-optimal
training \citep{kaplan2020,hoffmann2022}; our frontier transposes that question to inference. Early-exit
networks \citep{teerapittayanon2016} pursue exactly the per-instance adaptivity that
Sec.~\ref{sec:adaptive} bounds in this setting.
\paragraph{Test-time compute, verification, OOD.} Inference-time scaling \citep{snell2024} and energy-based
``thinking'' \citep{gladstone2025} motivate the compute-helps view we bound here. Energy is a common OOD
score \citep{liu2020,grathwohl2020}; our Sec.~\ref{sec:ood-sub} characterizes how that signal behaves
\emph{under} refinement, complementing reconstruction-based diffusion OOD detection, which exploits the same erasure
(``healing'') of anomalies \citep{graham2023}.

\section{Setup}
Given clean $\mathbf{x}$ and noise level $\sigma$, form $\mathbf{u}_0=\mathbf{x}+\sigma\epsilon$ and iterate
$\mathbf{u}_{k+1}=\mathbf{u}_k-\eta_k\nabla E_\theta(\mathbf{u}_k)$ with a decaying step size. Models are
trained by denoising score matching. Per image, $K^{*}=\arg\max_k\mathrm{PSNR}(\mathbf{u}_k,\mathbf{x})$; we
average over held-out data and fit $\log K^{*}=\log C+\alpha\log\sigma$. Unless noted, results use
$2$--$3$ seeds per configuration; we report means with standard deviations and, where stated, bootstrap
$95\%$ confidence intervals.

\section{A Calibrated Depth Law}
\label{sec:law}
\paragraph{Within-regime stability---and what the error bar means.} Over $25$ matched runs spanning three
datasets and five architectures, the fitted exponent is $\alpha=1.370\pm0.010$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:law}). The
$\pm0.010$ is run-to-run repeatability \emph{within one measurement protocol} (shared $\sigma$ grid,
step-size schedule, and per-seed noise realizations), not uncertainty about $\alpha$: a bootstrap over
runs gives $R^2=0.981$ with $95\%$ CI $[1.23,1.60]$, and protocol choices move the exponent by far more
than seed noise (below). Per dataset: CIFAR-10 $1.376\pm0.004$, Fashion-MNIST $1.372\pm0.005$, MNIST
$1.356\pm0.009$.
\paragraph{Architecture dependence is protocol-dependent.} Under the coarse matched protocol, CIFAR
architectures are indistinguishable (KAN $1.376$, GELU $1.378$, SiLU $1.379$, Tanh $1.373$; variance-ratio
$F=0.79$ with $3$ seeds per architecture). We caution against reading this as positive evidence of
equivalence: with $12$ fits the test has little power, architectures share noise realizations within each
seed index (which biases $F$ downward), and under a finer per-image protocol
(Sec.~\ref{sec:frontier}) the KAN and ConvMLP exponents separate cleanly ($1.365$ $[1.325,1.408]$ vs.\
$1.132$ $[1.083,1.184]$, pairwise bootstrap). What is robust across protocols is the \emph{form}
$K^{*}\propto\sigma^{\alpha}$; the exponent is a protocol- and regime-conditional quantity.

\paragraph{Scale robustness; the full exponent spread.} Table~\ref{tab:scale} extends the law over a
$3000\times$ range of model size. The \emph{form} is intact ($R^2>0.98$); the \emph{exponent} shifts to
$1.27$ for a globally-pooled energy trained on mixed noise. Beyond the matched regime the measured spread
is wider still: the same pretrained DDPM under noise-matched (rather than fixed) conditioning gives
$1.205$, and CelebA at $64$/$256$\,px gives $1.265$/$1.610$. Earlier internal analyses excluded these
runs from the headline mean as ``known deviations''; we report them because they delimit the claim: the
full measured spread across all settings we have run is $1.13$--$1.61$, and the tight $1.37$ describes
the matched low-resolution regime only.

\paragraph{One-point calibration, and what its error costs.} Fixing $\alpha=1.37$ and estimating $C$ from
a single $\sigma=0.16$ measurement predicts $K^{*}$ across the range with $7.5\%$ mean and $25\%$
worst-case error over $26$ runs (the $25$ matched runs plus the noise-matched DDPM, which contributes the
$25\%$ worst case). In \emph{delivered quality} the rule is forgiving, because the PSNR curve is flat near
its peak: on the frontier curves of Sec.~\ref{sec:frontier} the $\alpha=1.37$ rule costs at most
$0.115$\,dB versus the per-$\sigma$ oracle depth, and mis-setting $\alpha$ anywhere in $[1.13,1.6]$ costs
at most $0.35$\,dB. This robustness cuts both ways: it makes the rule deployable, and it bounds how much
exponent precision is worth inside the studied range.
\paragraph{Functional form.} Our $\sigma$ grid spans $0.78$ decades ($0.05$--$0.3$) with $K^{*}$ between
$1$ and ${\sim}10$; over so short a range a high $R^{2}$ does not discriminate a power law from other
smooth monotone forms, and the lowest-$\sigma$ point is floor-limited at $K^{*}{=}1$. We therefore treat
$C\sigma^{\alpha}$ as the simplest adequate description of the data, not an identified law of nature.

\section{Iteration, Not Architecture}
\label{sec:iter}
At $\sigma=0.2$, a feedforward denoiser trained with the same objective is exactly flat across $K$
($25.29$\,dB)---by construction, since a single-pass architecture has no gradient field to iterate. The
EBM shows semiconvergence: PSNR rises to $25.9$\,dB near $K=5$, then decays to $18.7$\,dB by $K=50$ as
over-denoising erases signal (Fig.~\ref{fig:semiconv}). Two qualifications keep this honest. First,
iteration is not quality supremacy: a \emph{parameter-matched} feedforward DnCNN ($33$K parameters)
reaches $27.4$\,dB at $\sigma=0.2$ in a single pass ($27.29$--$27.43$ over three training seeds)---above
the semiconvergence peak of every energy head we measure (max $26.6$\,dB). For fixed-noise denoising
quality alone, a well-trained feedforward model wins; what it lacks, and what this paper studies, is the
compute dial. Second, the flat baseline is flat definitionally; \emph{iterating} a feedforward denoiser
as a plug-and-play operator \citep{venkatakrishnan2013} would create its own $K$-dial and is outside our
scope. The capacity to convert compute into quality is a property of the iterative operator, not the
parameterization---and it is a dial, not a superiority claim.

iterative model is flat at a lower level. The $K$-dial exists only for iterative operators.}

\section{A Phase Boundary: Which Corruptions Admit Test-Time Compute}
\label{sec:phase}
The law presupposes that iteration helps at all---and it does not always. Sweeping five corruption types
across three architectures (Table~\ref{tab:phase}, Fig.~\ref{fig:phase}), we find a clean dichotomy. For
\emph{stochastic} corruptions---additive Gaussian, multiplicative speckle, and signal-dependent Poisson
noise---the depth law holds with $R^{2}\in[0.95,0.99]$ and $K^{*}$ grows with severity. For
\emph{deterministic} degradations---Gaussian blur and JPEG compression---it breaks: the optimal depth is
$K^{*}\!\approx\!0$ (a single step is best, or iteration never helps) and no power law fits
($R^{2}\le0.31$). The boundary tracks the training objective, not the corruption class per se: a
DSM-trained field models additive residuals, and while a deterministic degradation certainly leaves a
residual ($\mathbf{x}-h\!*\!\mathbf{x}\neq0$ for blur), that residual is structured and
signal-correlated---outside the span of what the score field was trained to remove. Inverting it requires
the forward operator (plug-and-play, RED, diffusion posterior sampling
\citep{venkatakrishnan2013,romano2017,chung2023}) or degradation-specific training, under which iterative
restoration of blur \emph{does} convert steps into quality \citep{bansal2022,delbracio2023}. We ran this
control: an identical energy head trained on blurred--clean pairs (a blur-residual objective replacing
the DSM noise residual) converts steps into quality on blur---$+0.9$ to $+2.8$\,dB over its single-pass
output, with $K^{*}$ growing from $6$ to beyond our $K{=}30$ sweep as severity increases---whereas the
DSM-trained heads give $K^{*}\!\approx\!0$. The boundary tracks the training objective, as claimed; we do
not fit a severity law for the control because its $K^{*}$ is cap-censored. Test-time compute for a
\emph{DSM-trained, prior-only} descent is thus corruption-conditioned: useful for stochastic corruptions,
inert for deterministic ones. (One caveat: the JPEG row is the weakest---its $K^{*}$ is $0$--$2$ and
weakly increasing---so the dichotomy rests mainly on blur.)

\section{The Inference Frontier: Trading Head Capacity for Depth}
\label{sec:frontier}
The depth law fixes how quality scales with iteration count for a given energy head. A deployment
question remains: at a \emph{fixed inference FLOP budget}, should one spend the budget on a heavier
head taking fewer descent steps, or a lighter head taking more? We measure this directly. For each
head we count the FLOPs of one descent step (one energy forward plus the input-gradient backward;
matmul/conv operations, counted with PyTorch's \texttt{FlopCounterMode}) and record PSNR after every
step ($K{=}0{\dots}30$, five noise levels, 500 CIFAR-10 test images, two noise seeds), yielding
quality-versus-cumulative-FLOPs curves for six energy heads (Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}). The comparison
criterion was \emph{pre-registered} before data collection: the lighter head must lead by
${\geq}0.3$\,dB over a contiguous budget range in at least three of five noise levels and in both
seeds, with the parameter-matched pair KAN-32K ($118.8$\,MFLOPs/step) vs.\ KAN-110K
($406.8$\,MFLOPs/step) primary.

The criterion passes---and an envelope analysis sharpens what ``substitution'' means. At
$\sigma\geq0.10$ the 32K head, given the 110K head's budget in extra steps, exceeds it over contiguous
budget ranges spanning $0.41$--$2.4$\,GFLOPs/image (every range begins at $406.8$\,MF---one KAN-110K
step, the earliest budget at which the pair is comparable), by margins that grow with noise: $+0.8$\,dB
at $\sigma{=}0.10$ to $+3.9$\,dB at $\sigma{=}0.30$, in both seeds ($4/5$ noise levels; the
pre-registered requirement was $3/5$). The advantage is not an artifact of marching the heavy head past
its optimum: at a $1.4$\,GFLOP budget slice ($\sigma{=}0.30$), the 110K head's best achievable PSNR at
\emph{any} depth within budget is ${\sim}19.7$\,dB versus the 32K head's $23.65$\,dB. The deeper fact
behind the frontier is that capacity buys almost nothing here: at every noise level the 110K head's
semiconvergence \emph{peak} exceeds the 32K head's by only $+0.04$ to $+0.12$\,dB, at $3.4\times$ the
per-step cost---the oversized head is Pareto-dominated. Both facts survive training stochasticity:
retraining both heads from scratch with two fresh training seeds under the matched recipe reproduces the
pre-registered verdict (PASS at $3/5$ noise levels in both evaluation seeds, for both training seeds)
with peak gaps in the same $+0.04$--$+0.12$\,dB band. They also survive scale: heads of $1$M, $8$M, and
$30$M parameters trained under the same recipe peak \emph{no higher} than the 32K head at any noise
level (within $-0.8$ to $-0.4$\,dB of it), while a single step of the $30$M head ($122.8$\,GF) costs
$34\times$ the 32K head's entire $30$-step trajectory. Under this recipe and data budget, parameter
count between $33$K and $30$M buys no peak quality at all---though we note matched epochs need not mean
matched convergence for the largest heads. Substitution is therefore bounded by the light
head's own peak (depth can never buy quality above it) and closes where the law leaves no depth to trade:
at $\sigma{=}0.05$, $K^{*}{\approx}1$ for both heads, the peak gap is $0.05$\,dB, and no
${\geq}0.3$\,dB advantage region exists in either direction---the criterion's one failure is a tie at
unequal cost, not a win for capacity. A grouped-KAN head in the style of \citet{yang2025kat}, at $8.3$K
parameters and $32.2$\,MFLOPs/step, extends the frontier to budgets the other heads cannot reach
(Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier}, leftmost curves).

Two scoping notes. \emph{Metric:} at each head's own optimal depth ($\sigma{=}0.10$), LPIPS is $0.0201$
(KAN-110K) vs.\ $0.0207$ (KAN-32K)---a perceptual tie, confirming both the budget-level PSNR advantage
and the peak-level near-tie are not metric artifacts. \emph{Cost model:} FLOPs price a serial resource as
if it were parallel. On our GPU the 32K head costs $0.469$\,ms/step versus $0.868$\,ms for the 110K head
($1.85\times$, not the $3.4\times$ FLOP ratio---KAN gather operations are memory-bound and uncounted by
\texttt{FlopCounterMode}, a bias favoring exactly the heads the FLOP frontier crowns), so the
within-family conclusion survives latency accounting in attenuated form. Cross-family FLOP comparisons do
\emph{not} transfer to latency: the ConvMLP head runs $15.7\times$ faster per step than KAN-32K despite
counting more FLOPs. Batch size matters too: at batch~$1$ every head is launch-bound
($2$--$7$\,ms/step regardless of size), so more steps means proportionally more latency and the frontier
inverts for latency-critical single-image serving; the ratios above hold in batched (throughput)
serving. We therefore scope the frontier to FLOP-budgeted (throughput-style) serving and
within-family comparisons, and report wall-clock alongside FLOPs. Composed with the depth law, the result
is a \emph{one-point-calibrated} deployment rule: pick the lightest head whose semiconvergence peak meets
the quality requirement, and let $K^{*}(\sigma)=C\sigma^{\alpha}$ set the steps---noting that $\sigma$
must be known or estimated at inference, and $C$ requires one measurement.

The grouped-KAN head also constitutes a sixth architecture on the depth law: trained under the
identical denoising-score-matching recipe, it follows $K^{*}(\sigma)=C\sigma^{\alpha}$ with
$R^{2}=0.995$ and $\alpha=1.206$ ($95\%$ CI $[1.13,1.29]$) under a per-image fine-grid protocol,
within the range that same protocol yields for the other heads ($1.13$--$1.43$). We do not pool
these fine-grid exponents with Table~\ref{tab:scale} or the $F$-test of Sec.~\ref{sec:law}, which
use a coarser matched protocol; the $F=0.79$ statistic remains scoped to the original five
architectures.

\section{Two Things Test-Time Compute Does Not Buy}
\label{sec:limits}
\subsection{No per-instance adaptive depth}
\label{sec:adaptive}
If $K^{*}$ varied enough across inputs, one could budget compute per image. The variation exists and
grows with noise: over $500$ images per level, the per-image $K^{*}$ has standard deviation $0.1$ steps
at $\sigma{=}0.05$, $0.5$ at $0.10$, $0.7$ at $0.15$, $1.3$ at $0.20$ (range $4$--$21$), and $2.0$ at
$0.30$ (range $3$--$30$). In every case the spread is small relative to the $1$--$11$ step range the law
traverses across $\sigma$, so the noise level, not the instance, carries the allocation signal. The
decisive quantity is what exploiting the spread could buy: an \emph{oracle} that stops each image at its
own best depth gains only $+0.12$, $+0.10$, and $+0.13$\,dB over the single mean-optimal depth at
$\sigma=0.10$, $0.20$, and $0.30$ respectively ($500$ images). Per-image depth control is therefore worth
at most ${\sim}0.13$\,dB here even with perfect information---about the same as the one-point rule's own
error ($0.115$\,dB, Sec.~\ref{sec:law}). Adaptive inference-depth allocation, at least for this setting,
is not where the leverage is.

\subsection{Refinement is information-dissipative}
\label{sec:ood-sub}
It is natural to expect the energy \emph{after} refinement to be a sharper confidence/OOD signal than the
raw input energy. In the regime the depth law governs, the reverse holds: far-OOD detectability by the
scalar energy (AUROC, CIFAR-10 vs.\ SVHN) declines monotonically with depth, $0.92$ at $K{=}0$ to $0.77$
at $K{=}30$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ood}); at deployable depths ($K{=}2$--$5$, where the law stops) the loss is
$0.5$--$3.5$ points. For semantically near OOD (CIFAR-100) the energy is at chance ($0.51$--$0.53$) at
\emph{every} depth, $K{=}0$ included---there the signal never existed, so nothing is dissipated. Descent
contracts every input toward the learned manifold; since the map is (generically) injective, what decays
is not information in the data-processing sense but the \emph{separability of the scalar energy readout}.
Two scope conditions matter. First, the effect is noise-conditioned: for heavily corrupted inputs
($\sigma{=}0.35$) the \emph{refined} energy beats the static one for far-OOD (AUROC $0.65\!\to\!0.78$,
consistent across four checkpoints)---when corruption swamps the static readout, refinement first strips
noise before it erases identity. Second, our energy is a generative, DSM-trained score, unlike the
supervised logsumexp energy of \citet{liu2020}; notably it does not exhibit the likelihood pathology of
\citet{nalisnick2019}, in which CIFAR-trained deep generative models rank SVHN as \emph{more}
in-distribution---it behaves as a distance to the learned manifold. Practically: on clean or lightly
corrupted inputs, score anomalies \emph{before} the model thinks; under heavy corruption, after a few
steps. This is consistent with reconstruction-based diffusion OOD detection, which succeeds by measuring
the erasure itself \citep{graham2023}.

\includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{fig_ood_decay}
\caption{Refinement erases the separability of the energy score. Far-OOD detectability decays
monotonically with descent depth; near-OOD detectability is at chance at every depth (no signal to
dissipate). On lightly corrupted inputs the best anomaly score is the energy \emph{before} refinement;
under heavy corruption ($\sigma{=}0.35$, not shown) the ordering reverses.}
\label{fig:ood}

\section{Practical Takeaways}
(1) To set inference depth, measure $K^{*}$ at one noise level and apply
$K^{*}(\sigma)=C\sigma^{\alpha}$ with the regime's exponent; mis-setting $\alpha$ within $[1.13,1.6]$
costs $\leq0.35$\,dB in-range, but re-fit when the architecture, recipe, or data change. (2) Do not
over-refine---quality peaks early and decays. (3) In FLOP-budgeted serving, prefer the lightest head
whose semiconvergence peak meets the quality bar and give it more steps; check wall-clock, which can
invert cross-family FLOP comparisons. (4) If fixed-noise quality is all that matters, a parameter-matched
feedforward denoiser may beat the iterative optimum---iterate for the compute dial, not for supremacy.
(5) Score anomalies with the input energy before refinement on lightly corrupted inputs (after a few
steps under heavy corruption), and do not expect per-image depth budgeting to add much over the
noise-level rule.

\section{Discussion: Inference Depth as Compute Allocation}
Our results are, at heart, statements about the \emph{value of computation}. $K^{*}(\sigma)$ is the
compute-optimal stopping point; the rise-then-decay of quality is structurally similar to the
\emph{over-thinking} reported for reasoning models, where accuracy first improves and then degrades with
longer chains \citep{chen2024overthink}, and to the allocation question of how test-time compute should
be spent \citep{snell2024}. The analogy is structural, not mechanistic: our iteration is a contractive
gradient flow at a known noise level, autoregressive decoding is neither, and our results transfer only
where those conditions hold. Our phase boundary sharpens the denoising side into a
value-of-computation criterion: iteration is worth spending only for stochastic corruptions and is inert
for deterministic ones---a structural predictor of \emph{when} thinking longer can help at all. This
connects the denoising setting to the broader effort to allocate test-time compute rationally, from
cost-aware ``rational metareasoning'' for language models \citep{ram2024} to energy-minimization views of
reasoning where the energy itself supplies a termination criterion \citep{du2022}. It also echoes a
normative principle from cognitive science and neuroscience---that agents allocate limited computational
(indeed metabolic) resources against their expected value \citep{lieder2020,christie2015}. We do not claim
a normative derivation here; rather, our empirical law, its calibration, and its corruption-conditioned
boundary supply concrete, measured constraints that such a theory of optimal inference-time compute would
need to reproduce.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
``Universal'' describes the law's form, not its exponent. The study is confined to additive-noise
denoising at low resolution; the largest model is probed, not trained under our protocol; the dissipation
result concerns energy-based/reconstruction inference specifically; and the substitution frontier is
measured on one dataset (CIFAR-10) and within one model family per comparison; its verdict is replicated
across three independent training-seed pairs, and its scale extension ($1$M--$30$M heads) shares one
recipe whose epoch budget may undertrain the largest heads.
A spectral-shrinkage mechanism we hypothesized for the exponent
($\alpha=2\gamma/\beta$) failed its direct test---a Gaussian-random-field sweep measured slope
$-0.09\pm0.44$ against a predicted $-1$---so the mechanism behind the scaling remains open; we report
this negative result in the same spirit as the others. We make no claim that energy descent provides
adaptive compute or calibrated confidence; we show it does not, here.

\section{Conclusion}
Iterative denoisers obey a simple, scale-robust, calibratable depth law with a protocol- and
regime-conditional exponent, and the compute dial is intrinsic to iteration; at matched inference
budgets, depth substitutes for head capacity wherever the law leaves depth to trade, and oversized heads
are Pareto-dominated. The boundaries are equally concrete: the substitution closes into a cost-tie at low
noise, per-instance depth variation adds little over the noise-level rule, and refinement erases the
separability of the energy score on lightly corrupted inputs. Both the rule and its limits should help
practitioners decide how long to let an iterative model think---and when not to bother.
